# Soil moisture

Soil moisture is the water held in the pore space of the soil. It matters because crops take up water through their roots from that storage, and because the amount of water present controls how much the plant must transpire, how nutrients move to the roots, and how well roots can grow.

Two reference points describe the water available to plants. At the wilting point the soil holds water so tightly that roots can no longer extract enough to keep the plant functioning; at field capacity the soil has drained freely and holds the water it can readily supply. The water between these two limits is the plant-available water, and its size depends strongly on soil texture: sandy soils hold little, clay and silt soils hold much more.

Soil moisture changes daily with rainfall, irrigation, evaporation from the soil surface, transpiration by the crop and drainage below the root zone. This balance is the basis of irrigation scheduling: applying water when the root zone has used a set fraction of its available water, and refilling without over-irrigating, which would only drain below the roots and can carry salts and nutrients away.

Soil moisture can be observed from space. NASA's SMAP mission measures surface soil moisture in roughly the top five centimetres, on a grid of about nine kilometres, using a radiometer. Those values describe a region rather than a field, they represent only the surface layer, and quality flags must be respected; root-zone moisture is not measured directly by SMAP and is only available from model-derived products.
