# Soil health and crop sequence effects

Soil health describes how well a soil functions: it holds and transmits water, supports roots, cycles nutrients, resists erosion and buffers against stress. Organic matter, aggregation, biological activity and the balance of pore sizes all contribute, and they change slowly, over seasons and years.

Crop sequence affects soil health through the amount and quality of residues returned, the depth and pattern of rooting, the period during which the soil is covered, and the frequency of tillage and traffic. A sequence that keeps living roots or residue cover in the ground for more of the year generally protects the surface from erosion and heat and feeds soil organisms, whereas long bare periods leave the surface exposed.

Diversity is a common recommendation: alternating crop families, mixing deep and shallow roots, and including legumes and high-residue crops. The benefits accumulate and are hard to measure in a single season, so a decision-support tool should present them as directional rather than as guaranteed outcomes.

Soil health cannot be inferred from a vegetation index or from a single soil moisture reading. Assessing it needs soil tests (organic matter, texture, pH, salinity) and observations in the field. This project therefore reports soil-health effects as qualitative indicators and always asks for local verification.
