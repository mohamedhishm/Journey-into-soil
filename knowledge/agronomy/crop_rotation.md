# Crop rotation

Crop rotation is the practice of growing different crops in a planned sequence on the same field over time, instead of repeating the same crop season after season. The sequence is chosen so that the crops differ in family, rooting depth, season and nutrient demand.

Rotation is used to interrupt pest and disease cycles that build up when the same host crop is grown repeatedly, to spread water and nutrient demand across the season, and to keep the soil covered for more of the year. Growing crops from different families in succession is the usual recommendation, because crops of one family tend to share pests, diseases and nutrient needs.

A rotation does not need to be complex to be useful. A simple alternation between a cereal and a legume, or between a shallow-rooted and a deep-rooted crop, already changes the pest cycle and the pattern of water use compared with continuous cropping.

Rotation planning has limits. It cannot by itself fix a drainage or salinity problem, it does not replace a soil test, and the best sequence depends on the local market, water supply, labour and the farmer's own experience. A plan that looks good on paper still needs local verification.
