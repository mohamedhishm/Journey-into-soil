# Legumes and nitrogen fixation

Legumes such as clover, faba bean, lentil, cowpea and soybean can form a symbiosis with Rhizobium bacteria in root nodules. The bacteria convert atmospheric nitrogen gas into forms the plant can use, a process called biological nitrogen fixation. Because the nitrogen comes from the air rather than from fertilizer, a legume can leave more plant-available nitrogen in the field for the following crop.

The amount of nitrogen fixed is not fixed. It depends on the legume species, how long the crop grows, whether the right Rhizobium strain is present (often requiring inoculation of the seed), soil moisture, temperature, and how much nitrogen is already available in the soil. Published cover-crop ranges for legumes are typically broad, on the order of tens to about a hundred and fifty pounds of nitrogen per acre, and they are not a guaranteed credit.

Legumes also affect soil in other ways: their roots and residues add organic matter, they can improve aggregation and water infiltration, and deep-rooted species can loosen compacted layers. These effects build up over years of use rather than in a single season.

Practical cautions matter. A legume that is terminated early fixes less nitrogen. Residues with a high carbon-to-nitrogen ratio can temporarily tie up nitrogen while they decompose. Legumes can also host their own pests and diseases, so they need to be placed in the rotation rather than grown continuously. In this project, legume nitrogen effects are treated as qualitative and no fertilizer rates are recommended.
